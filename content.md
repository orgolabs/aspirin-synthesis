## One aromatic core

Salicylic acid and aspirin share the same benzene-ring core carrying a carboxylic acid — this experiment only changes the *periphery* of that core. That makes aspirin a friendly first look at how chemists turn one useful molecule into another.

**The reaction (acetylation / esterification):** salicylic acid is heated briefly with **acetic anhydride** and a drop of acid catalyst, which acetylates its phenol –OH to give **aspirin** plus acetic acid. Aspirin itself (first marketed by Bayer in 1899) is one of the most widely used medicines in the world.

## 2 · Mechanism &amp; pathway

The aspirin-forming step is a **nucleophilic acyl substitution** (an esterification): the phenol oxygen of salicylic acid attacks a carbonyl carbon of acetic anhydride, and acetate leaves.

<div data-mount="pathway"></div>

<div class="mech-grid">
<article class="mech-step"><span class="step-num">1</span>
<h3>Activate the carbonyl</h3>
<p>A trace of acid catalyst (85% $\ce{H3PO4}$) protonates a carbonyl of <strong>acetic anhydride</strong>, making its carbon more electrophilic — easier for a weak nucleophile like a phenol to attack.</p>
</article>
<article class="mech-step major-accent"><span class="step-num">2</span>
<h3>Phenol attacks → ester</h3>
<p>The <strong>phenol –OH</strong> of salicylic acid attacks that carbonyl carbon. The anhydride breaks, transferring a <strong>–COCH₃ (acetyl)</strong> group to the oxygen and forming the new <strong>ester</strong> bond of aspirin.</p>
</article>
<article class="mech-step minor-accent"><span class="step-num">3</span>
<h3>Acetic acid leaves</h3>
<p>The other half of the anhydride departs as <strong>acetic acid</strong>, and the catalyst is regenerated. Net: salicylic acid + acetic anhydride → aspirin + acetic acid.</p>
</article>
</div>

<div class="callout info">
<h3>Why acetic anhydride instead of acetic acid?</h3>
<p>You could esterify with acetic acid directly, but that equilibrium is slow and unfavorable. <strong>Acetic anhydride</strong> is a much better acetylating agent: its leaving group (acetate/acetic acid) makes the acylation fast and essentially irreversible, so the reaction finishes in minutes. Excess anhydride is destroyed with water at the end (it hydrolyzes to harmless acetic acid).</p>
</div>

<div class="callout">
<h3>Reading it by IR (how you prove it worked)</h3>
<p>Salicylic acid shows a broad hydrogen-bonded <strong>O–H band (≈3000–3500 cm⁻¹)</strong> and a single C=O near <strong>1700 cm⁻¹</strong>. <strong>Aspirin loses the broad phenol O–H</strong> (that oxygen is now an ester) and shows <strong>two distinct C=O peaks</strong> — one ester, one carboxylic acid. That change, together with the melting point, is the fingerprint that the acetylation happened.</p>
</div>

## 3 · Procedure

The one-step **microscale** procedure from Williamson &amp; Masters, *Macroscale and Microscale Organic Experiments* (7th ed., Cengage, p. 534). Always use your instructor's version and your lab's safety rules — this is a study aid, not a substitute for a supervised SOP.

<a class="dl-btn" href="docs/synthesis-of-aspirin-lab-manual.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">⬇ Download the full lab handout (PDF)</a>

| Reagent | Amount | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Salicylic acid | 138 mg (≈1.0 mmol) | substrate (the phenol/acid) |
| Acetic anhydride | 0.3 mL = 5 drops (≈0.32 g, 3.2 mmol, ~3 equiv) | acetylating agent (excess) |
| 85% phosphoric acid, $\ce{H3PO4}$ | 1 drop | acid catalyst |
| Deionized water | a few drops | quench &amp; (optional) recrystallize |

A boiling chip goes in the reaction tube with the salicylic acid.

### Salicylic acid → aspirin (acetylation)

1. Weigh **138 mg salicylic acid** into a reaction tube and add a **boiling chip**.
2. In the **fume hood**, add **1 small drop of 85% $\ce{H3PO4}$** followed by **0.3 mL (5 drops) acetic anhydride** (caustic vapors — the anhydride also washes the reactants to the bottom of the tube). **Mix thoroughly.**
3. Heat the tube in a **water bath at ~90 °C for 5 min**.
4. Cautiously add **0.2 mL (3 drops) water** to decompose the excess acetic anhydride — this is **exothermic**.
5. Add a further **0.3 mL (5 drops) water** and let the tube cool **slowly to room temperature**.
6. If crystals do not form on cooling, add a **seed crystal** or **scratch** the inside of the tube with a glass stirring rod.
7. **Cool in ice** until crystallization is complete (**at least 10 min**), then remove the solvent with a **Pasteur pipette**. If the crystals are too fine for this, collect the product by **vacuum filtration on a Hirsch funnel**, completing the transfer with a little ice water.
8. Turn the product onto filter paper and **squeeze it between sheets of filter paper** to absorb excess water; **air-dry thoroughly**.
9. **Weigh** the dry product and calculate **percent yield** (calculator below); determine the **melting point** (lit. 128–137 °C) and an **IR spectrum**.

<div class="callout">
<h3>Optional recrystallization</h3>
<p>Aspirin is hydrolyzed by boiling water, but the reaction is not rapid. If a purer product is wanted, recrystallize from a <strong>small quantity of very hot water</strong>, then cool, isolate, and dry.</p>
</div>

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety:</strong> acetic anhydride gives caustic vapors — work in a fume hood; 85% $\ce{H3PO4}$ is corrosive. Step 4 (water quench) is exothermic — add slowly. Goggles and gloves. <strong>Cleaning up:</strong> dilute the filtrate with water and flush it down the drain (or follow your lab's waste rules).</div>
