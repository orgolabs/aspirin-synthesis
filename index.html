The Synthesis of Aspirin

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is made by acetylating the phenol group of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride. In the classic general-chemistry version it is the second half of a two-step journey that starts from oil of wintergreen — a single aromatic core, modified step by step. One aromatic core, modified twice

Oil of wintergreen (methyl salicylate), salicylic acid, and aspirin all share the same benzene-ring core carrying a carbonyl group — the reactions in this experiment only change the periphery of that core. That makes aspirin a friendly first look at how chemists build a useful molecule from a natural starting material.

Step 1 (hydrolysis): oil of wintergreen is boiled with NaOH and then acidified, cleaving its ester to give salicylic acid. Step 2 (condensation / esterification): salicylic acid is heated with acetic anhydride and a drop of acid catalyst, which acetylates its phenol –OH to give aspirin plus acetic acid. Aspirin itself (first marketed by Bayer in 1899) is one of the most widely used medicines in the world.

2 · How the acetyl group is installed

The aspirin-forming step is a nucleophilic acyl substitution (an esterification): the phenol oxygen of salicylic acid attacks a carbonyl carbon of acetic anhydride, and acetate leaves.

Reaction pathway (two steps)

Step 1 hydrolyses the natural ester to salicylic acid; step 2 acetylates its phenol –OH to aspirin. The mechanism of that second step is below.

1

Activate the carbonyl

A trace of acid catalyst (conc. H₃PO₄) protonates a carbonyl of acetic anhydride, making its carbon more electrophilic — easier for a weak nucleophile like a phenol to attack.

2

Phenol attacks → ester

The phenol –OH of salicylic acid attacks that carbonyl carbon. The anhydride breaks, transferring a –COCH₃ (acetyl) group to the oxygen and forming the new ester bond of aspirin.

3

Acetic acid leaves

The other half of the anhydride departs as acetic acid, and the catalyst is regenerated. Net: salicylic acid + acetic anhydride → aspirin + acetic acid.

Why acetic anhydride instead of acetic acid?

You could imagine esterifying with acetic acid directly, but that equilibrium is slow and unfavorable. Acetic anhydride is a much better acetylating agent: its leaving group (acetate/acetic acid) makes the acylation fast and essentially irreversible, so the reaction finishes in minutes. Excess anhydride is destroyed with water at the end (it hydrolyzes to harmless acetic acid).

Reading it by FTIR (how you prove it worked)

Salicylic acid and oil of wintergreen show a broad hydrogen-bonded O–H band (≈3000–3500 cm⁻¹) and a single C=O near 1700 cm⁻¹. Aspirin loses the broad O–H (the phenol is now an ester) and shows two distinct C=O peaks — one for the ester and one for the carboxylic acid. That change is the fingerprint that the acetylation happened.

Step 1 — Oil of wintergreen → salicylic acid (hydrolysis)

  1. Weigh ~4 mL oil of wintergreen into a clean, dry 250-mL beaker.
  2. Add 40 mL of 6 M NaOH with stirring (a precipitate forms); heat to a gentle boil for 15 min, reducing the rate to avoid bumping.
  3. Cool in an ice bath until just warm, then slowly add 50 mL of 8 M H₂SO₄ with stirring (salicylic acid precipitates).
  4. Chill, then isolate the solid by suction filtration (Büchner funnel); rinse with iced deionized water.
  5. Recrystallize: dissolve the crude solid in ~100 mL hot deionized water, cool, chill in ice, isolate, and dry overnight.

Step 2 — Salicylic acid → aspirin (acetylation)

  1. Weigh 1.4 g salicylic acid into a dry 125-mL Erlenmeyer flask.
  2. In the fume hood, add 3.0 mL acetic anhydride (caustic vapors) and 5 drops conc. H₃PO₄.
  3. Stopper with a one-hole stopper fitted with tubing; float the flask in a water bath and hold at 85–90 °C for 5 min (do not boil).
  4. Stop heating and immediately add 2 mL water through the tubing to destroy excess acetic anhydride (hot acid vapors).
  5. Cool, remove the stopper, add 20 mL water; let stand until crystals form, then add 10 mL water, swirl, and chill in an ice bath.
  6. Isolate the crude aspirin by suction filtration; rinse with iced water.
  7. Recrystallize: dissolve in ~10 mL water per gram of solid, heat to dissolve, cool and chill to crystallize, and isolate.
  8. Oven-dry at 80 °C for 1 h (aspirin melts/decomposes at ~100 °C), cool, and weigh.

Characterization & report

  1. Record the mass of product and calculate theoretical and percent yield (use the calculator below).
  2. Take an FTIR spectrum — confirm loss of the broad O–H and the appearance of two C=O peaks in aspirin.
  3. Optionally measure the melting point of salicylic acid and aspirin.
  4. In your discussion, explain why isolated yields fall short of theoretical (losses on transfer, incomplete reaction, solubility during recrystallization).
Safety: Acetic anhydride gives off caustic vapors — use a fume hood. Concentrated H₃PO₄ and H₂SO₄ are corrosive; 6 M NaOH is caustic. Keep the aspirin oven below 100 °C (it melts and decomposes). Wear goggles and gloves.
Waste disposal: The acidic filtrate from Step 1 goes in the designated acidic aqueous waste. Other relatively benign liquid waste may be rinsed down the sink per your lab's rules.
